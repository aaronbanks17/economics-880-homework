    \section*{Part I}

        We solve for the competitive equilibrium in the setting of Hugget (1993) with enforceable insurance markets and 
        and no initial asset holdings. There are a unit measure of identical agents
        with preferences $\E_0[\sum_t\beta^tU(c_t)]$ where $U:\R_+ \to \R$ is strictly increasing, strictly concave, differentiable
        and bounded on its domain. As usualy, $\beta\in (0,1)$. The support of income shocks is $S =\{u, e\}$ where income is 
        $y:S\to\mathbb{R}$ with $y(e) = 1$ and $y(u) =b < 1$. Shocks are iid across agents and follow a Markov process: 
        \[
            \pi(s' \mid s) = \pr(s_{t+1} = s' \mid s_t = s).
        \]
        Each period, agents' income is realized, and they choose aseet holdings $a_{t+1} \geq \underline{a}$ 
        given a price $q_t \in [0,1]$ with consumption $c_t$ determine by the resiudal income from asset holding
        decisions and where $\underline{a} \leq 0$.

        We solve for the competitive equilibrium by first solving a planner's problem that weights each agent equally, and then
        decentralizing the planner's allocation via a bond price. While agent's are subject to income risk, in aggregate the Law 
        of Large Numbers implies now aggregate uncertainty. Thus, the planner's problem is given by 
        \begin{align*}
            \max_{\{(c_t^e, c_t^u)\}} \sum_{t=0}^\infty \beta^t \Big[\pi^e U(c_t^e) +  \pi^u U(c_t^u) \Big]  
            \qquad s.t. \qquad \pi^e y(e) + \pi^u y(u) \leq \pi^e c_t^e + \pi^u c_t^u
        \end{align*}
        where $\pi^s$ is the fraction of the population that receives income shock $s$ each period. With multiplier $\lambda_t$ 
        on the period $t$ resource feasibility constraint, the first order optimality conditons of the associated Lagrangian are 
        \begin{align*}
            \beta^t U'(c_t^s) - \lambda_t &= 0 \qquad s = e, u\\
            \lambda_t [\pi^e (c_t^e - y(e)) + \pi^u (c_t^u - y(u))] &= 0
        \end{align*}
        Strict concavity of the utility function implies that $c_t^e = c_t^u = \pi^e y(e) + \pi^u y(u)$ for all $t$. In other words, there is perfect
        consumption smoothing between those agents suffering different income shocks. 

        The household problem is 
        \begin{align*}
            \max_{\{(c_t, a_t)\}} \E_0 \sum_{t=0}^\infty \beta^t U(c_t(s_t)) 
            \qquad s.t. \qquad 
            c_t + \sum_{s \in S} Q(s \mid s_t) a_{t+1}(s, s_t) &\leq y(s_t) + a_t (s_t, s_{t-1})\\
            a_0 &= 0
        \end{align*}
        The consumption Euler equation for this problem is
        \[
            Q(s' \mid s) U'(c_t(s)) = \beta \pi(s' \mid s) U'(c_{t+1}(s')).
        \]
        To match the planner's allocation, the marginal utilities of consumption in adjacent periods must be equal as consumption is 
        smoothed across time and states. Hence, 
        \[
            Q(s' \mid s) = \beta \pi(s' \mid s),
        \]
        implying that the risk free rate is 
        \[
            q \equiv \sum_{s' \in S}Q(s' \mid s) = \beta.
        \]


    \section*{Part II}

        Figure \ref{fig:policy_functions} plots the equilibrium policy functions
        for employed and unemployed households. For employed households, the
        policy function first satisfies $g(a,e)<a$ at approximately $a=1.4404$.
        For unemployed households, the policy function first satisfies
        $g(a,u)<a$ at approximately $a=-1.9930$. Thus, for both earnings states,
        there exist asset levels $\bar{a}$ such that
        \[
            g(\bar{a},s)<\bar{a},
        \]
        verifying that households do not continue to accumulate assets at the
        upper end of the asset grid.

        The equilibrium bond price is $q^* = 0.9943.$
        At the reported equilibrium price, aggregate excess asset demand is
        approximately $0.00118$, which is close to the zero-net-supply
        market-clearing condition. Because asset choices are restricted to a
        grid of 1,000 points, the numerical excess-demand function might only be roughly
        equal zero. We therefore terminate the bisection algorithm when
        the price bracket becomes sufficiently small rather than requiring
        excess asset demand itself to equal zero.

        Figure \ref{fig:wealth_distributions} plots the cross-sectional wealth
        distributions for employed and unemployed households, where, wealth is defined 
        as current earnings plus net asset
        holdings, $w = s+a.$
        
        The stationary distribution assigns approximately $94.34\%$ of
        households to the employed state and $5.66\%$ to the unemployed state.
        Mean wealth in the stationary distribution is approximately $0.9729$.

        Figure \ref{fig:lorenz_curve} plots the Lorenz curve implied by the
        stationary wealth distribution. Because some households have negative
        net wealth, the Lorenz curve initially falls below zero before rising
        toward one. The resulting Gini coefficient is
        \[
            G = 0.3854.
        \]
        This is substantially below the wealth Gini of $0.80$ observed in the
        data. Thus, despite generating endogenous wealth inequality through
        uninsurable idiosyncratic earnings risk, the benchmark incomplete-markets
        model generates only about half of the wealth inequality observed in the
        data. Our estimate is also nearly identical to the benchmark model Gini
        of $0.38$ reported in the lecture notes.

    \section*{Part III}

    Figure \ref{fig:welfare_gains} plots the consumption-equivalent welfare
    gain $\lambda(a,s)$ from moving from the incomplete-markets equilibrium
    to the complete-markets allocation. The welfare gain is positive for
    households with sufficiently low asset holdings and becomes negative
    for households with sufficiently high asset holdings. Thus, poorer
    households tend to prefer the complete-markets allocation, while
    wealthier households tend to prefer the incomplete-markets equilibrium.

    Under complete markets, consumption is constant across households and
    states and is equal to average earnings,
    \[
        c^{FB}
        =
        \pi_e e + \pi_u u
        =
        0.971698.
    \]
    The corresponding lifetime utility is
    \[
        W^{FB}
        =
        -4.252524.
    \]

    Under incomplete markets, average lifetime utility is
    \[
        W^{INC}
        =
        \sum_{a,s} \mu(a,s)v(a,s)
        =
        -4.454135.
    \]
    Thus, average welfare is higher under complete markets than under
    incomplete markets.

    For each state $(a,s)$, the consumption-equivalent welfare gain is
    computed as
    \[
        \lambda(a,s)
        =
        \left[
            \frac{
                W^{FB}
                +
                \frac{1}{(1-\alpha)(1-\beta)}
            }{
                v(a,s)
                +
                \frac{1}{(1-\alpha)(1-\beta)}
            }
        \right]^{\frac{1}{1-\alpha}}
        -1.
    \]
    Averaging these gains over the stationary distribution gives
    \[
        WG
        =
        \sum_{a,s}
        \lambda(a,s)\mu(a,s)
        =
        0.001358.
    \]
    Therefore, the average household would be willing to give up
    approximately
    \[
        100\times WG
        =
        0.1358\%
    \]
    of consumption in every future period in order to obtain the
    complete-markets allocation.

    Finally, the fraction of the stationary population that weakly prefers
    the complete-markets allocation is
    \[
        \sum_{a,s}
        \mathbf{1}\{\lambda(a,s)\geq 0\}\mu(a,s)
        =
        0.540062.
    \]
    Thus, approximately $54.01\%$ of households would favor moving from
    the incomplete-markets equilibrium to complete markets.
